# LEVEL 1 — BASIC

## 1.1 What Is a Bond?

A bond is essentially a **loan**. When a company (or government) issues a bond, they are borrowing money from you, the investor, and promising to:
1. Pay you periodic interest (called the **coupon**)
2. Return your original investment (the **principal** or **face value**) on a set date (**maturity**)

Think of it like lending a friend money: they pay you back with interest, on a schedule you both agreed to. 
A corporate bond works the same way — except the "friend" is a company like Reliance, Apple, or Tata Motors, 
and the loan terms are formalized in a legal document called the **indenture/contract/terms - legal agreement**.

**Real-world example:** A company issues a bond with a face value of ₹1,000, a coupon rate of 7%, and a 5-year maturity. 
You pay ₹1,000 today. Each year, the company pays you ₹70 in interest. After 5 years, you get your ₹1,000 back.

## 1.2 Why Do Companies Issue Bonds?

Companies need capital to expand, refinance old debt, fund acquisitions, or manage cash flow. They have two main funding choices:
- **Equity** (selling ownership/shares) — no repayment obligation, but dilutes ownership
- **Debt** (issuing bonds/loans) — must be repaid with interest, but ownership stays intact

Bonds are attractive to companies because interest payments are tax-deductible, and bondholders don't get voting rights or a share of profits 
beyond the fixed interest.

## 1.3 Why Do Investors Buy Corporate Bonds?

- **Predictable income** — fixed coupon payments on a known schedule
- **Capital preservation** — principal returned at maturity (assuming no default)
- **Diversification** — bonds often behave differently than stocks, smoothing overall portfolio returns
- **Priority in bankruptcy** — bondholders get paid before shareholders if a company fails

## 1.4 Core Terminology (Level 1 Glossary)

| Term | Plain-English Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Face Value/Par Value** | The amount the bond repays at maturity (commonly ₹1,000 or $1,000) |
| **Coupon Rate** 		| The fixed annual interest rate paid, as a % of face value |
| **Maturity Date** 	| The date the company repays the principal |
| **Issuer** 			| The company borrowing the money |
| **Bondholder** 		| You — the lender/investor |
| **Yield** 			| The actual return you earn, which can differ from the coupon rate |
| **Credit Rating** 	| A grade (like AAA, BBB, etc.) indicating how likely the issuer is to repay |
| **Default** 			| When the issuer fails to pay interest or principal |

## 1.5 How Fund Managers Actually Use This

When I evaluate a new bond, the first three questions I ask are always:
1. **Who is the issuer**, and how strong is their business?
2. **What am I being paid** (coupon) relative to the risk I'm taking?
3. **When do I get my money back** (maturity), and does that fit my portfolio's needs?

Everything else — credit analysis, duration, spread — builds on these three basics.

# LEVEL 2 — INTERMEDIATE

## 2.1 Bond Pricing — Why Bonds Don't Always Trade at Face Value

Bonds trade on the open market after issuance, and their price moves **inversely to interest rates**. 
This is the single most important relationship in fixed income.

**Why?** If you hold a bond paying a 5% coupon, and new bonds start being issued at 7% (because interest rates rose), 
your 5% bond becomes less attractive. Its price must fall so that its *effective yield* to a new buyer rises to match the market.

| Interest Rate Direction | Bond Price Direction |
|---					  |---|
| Rates rise ↑ | Bond prices fall ↓ |
| Rates fall ↓ | Bond prices rise ↑ |

## 2.2 Yield — The Concept That Matters More Than Coupon

| Yield Type | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Coupon Yield** 		| The fixed rate printed on the bond (never changes) |
| **Current Yield** 	| Annual coupon ÷ current market price |
| **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** | The total annualized return if held to maturity, accounting for price paid, coupon, and time — the most 
								important yield figure professionals use |
| **Yield to Call (YTC)** | Same as YTM, but assumes the bond is redeemed early at the issuer's option (relevant for callable bonds) |

**Real-world example:** You buy a bond below face value (say ₹950 for a ₹1,000 face value bond) with a 6% coupon. 
Your current yield is higher than 6% (₹60/₹950 = 6.3%), and your YTM is higher still, because you'll also gain ₹50 in capital appreciation by maturity.

## 2.3 Credit Ratings — The Language of Risk

Rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA, S&P, Moody's, Fitch) grade issuers on their ability to repay debt.

| Rating Band 			| Category 					| What It Signals |
|---					|---						|---|
| AAA to BBB- (or Baa3) | **Investment Grade** 		| Lower risk, lower yield |
| BB+ and below 		| **High Yield / "Junk"** 	| Higher risk, higher yield to compensate |

**Key principle:** Lower credit quality = higher yield demanded by investors, to compensate for greater default risk. 
This extra yield is called the **credit spread**.

## 2.4 The Three Core Risks in Fixed Income

| Risk Type | What It Is | Real Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest Rate Risk** | Bond prices fall when rates rise | A 10-year bond bought before a rate hike cycle loses value |
| **Credit Risk** | Issuer may default or get downgraded | A company's earnings collapse, rating agency downgrades it, bond price drops |
| **Liquidity Risk** | Difficulty selling the bond quickly without a price discount | A small, obscure corporate bond with few buyers in the secondary market |

## 2.5 Fixed Income Mutual Funds — How They Work

A **bond mutual fund** pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of bonds, managed professionally.

| Fund Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Corporate Bond Fund** | Invests mainly in company-issued bonds (our focus area) |
| **Gilt Fund** | Invests in government securities only — near-zero credit risk |
| **Short Duration Fund** | Holds bonds with short maturities — lower interest rate risk |
| **Credit Risk Fund** | Deliberately holds lower-rated bonds for higher yield |
| **Dynamic Bond Fund** | Manager actively shifts duration based on rate outlook |

## 2.6 Key Fund Metrics You Must Understand

| Metric | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **NAV (Net Asset Value)** | Per-unit price of the fund, calculated daily |
| **Expense Ratio** | Annual fee charged by the fund, as % of assets |
| **Modified Duration** | How much the fund's value moves for a 1% change in interest rates (see Level 3) |
| **Average Maturity** | Weighted average time to maturity of all bonds in the fund |
| **Portfolio Credit Quality** | Breakdown of holdings by rating (e.g., 60% AAA, 30% AA, 10% A) |

## 2.7 How Fund Managers Actually Use This

At this stage, I'm not just picking one bond — I'm building a **portfolio**. I look at a fund's fact sheet and immediately check: 
1.Average maturity (rate risk), 
2.Credit quality breakdown (default risk), and 
3.Expense ratio (cost drag on returns). 

These three numbers tell me 80% of what I need to know before digging deeper.

# LEVEL 3 — ADVANCED

## 3.1 Duration — Measuring Interest Rate Sensitivity Precisely

**Duration** measures how sensitive a bond's (or fund's) price is to interest rate changes, expressed in years but functioning as a sensitivity measure.

| Duration Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Macaulay Duration** | The weighted-average time until all cash flows (coupons + principal) are received |
| **Modified Duration** | Macaulay Duration adjusted to directly estimate % price change per 1% rate change |

**Rule of thumb:** If a bond fund has a modified duration of 5, and interest rates rise by 1%, the fund's value will fall by approximately 5%.

**Real-world example:** A fund manager expecting rates to rise will **shorten duration** 
(sell long-maturity bonds, buy short-maturity ones) to protect the portfolio from price declines. 
A manager expecting rate cuts will **extend duration** to maximize price gains.

## 3.2 Convexity — The Refinement Duration Misses

Duration assumes a straight-line relationship between yield changes and price changes, but the real relationship is curved. **Convexity** measures this curvature.

- **Positive convexity** (typical for standard bonds) means prices rise *more* than duration predicts when yields fall, and fall *less* than duration predicts 
when yields rise — a favorable asymmetry for the investor.
- Bonds with **embedded options** (like callable bonds) can exhibit **negative convexity**, which is unfavorable.

## 3.3 Credit Analysis — Going Beyond the Rating

Professional fund managers don't just rely on published ratings — they do independent credit analysis:

| Analysis Area | What's Examined |
|---|---|
| **Financial Ratios** 		| Debt/EBITDA, Interest Coverage Ratio, Free Cash Flow |
| **Industry Position** 	| Market share, competitive moat, cyclicality |
| **Covenant Structure** 	| Legal protections in the bond indenture (e.g., restrictions on further borrowing) |
| **Management Quality** 	| Track record, capital allocation discipline |
| **Macro Sensitivity** 	| How exposed the issuer is to commodity prices, currency, or rate cycles |

## 3.4 Credit Spread Analysis

The **credit spread** is the extra yield a corporate bond offers over a "risk-free" government bond of the same maturity.

```
Credit Spread = Corporate Bond Yield − Government Bond Yield (same maturity)
```

Spreads **widen** during economic stress (investors demand more compensation for risk) and **narrow** during economic optimism. 
Watching spread movements is one of the most important real-time signals a fixed income manager tracks — it often moves before credit ratings are officially changed.

## 3.5 The Yield Curve and Its Signals

The yield curve plots yields across different maturities (e.g., 1-year, 5-year, 10-year, 30-year).

| Curve Shape | What It Typically Signals |
|---					|---|
| **Normal (upward sloping)** | Healthy economic growth expectations |
| **Flat** 		| Uncertainty or a transition phase |
| **Inverted (downward sloping)** | Often signals expected economic slowdown or recession — short-term rates exceed long-term rates |

## 3.6 Portfolio Construction Principles

| Principle | Application |
|---|---|
| **Laddering** | Buying bonds across staggered maturities to reduce reinvestment and rate risk |
| **Barbell Strategy** | Combining short-term and long-term bonds, avoiding the middle |
| **Bullet Strategy** | Concentrating maturities around a single target date (useful for matching a known future liability) |
| **Diversification by Issuer/Sector** | Avoiding overconcentration in any single company or industry |

## 3.7 How Fund Managers Actually Use This

At this level, I'm running scenario analysis constantly: "If rates rise 100bps, what happens to my NAV given my duration and convexity profile?" I'm also independently modeling issuer credit risk rather than trusting ratings blindly — ratings lag reality, especially in fast-deteriorating credits. Spread-widening is often my earliest warning signal, well before a downgrade is announced.

# LEVEL 4 — EXPERT / INSTITUTIONAL

## 4.1 Total Return Attribution

Institutional managers break down fund performance into distinct sources to understand *why* returns happened, not just what they were:

| Attribution Component | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Income Return** | Return from coupon payments |
| **Price Return (Rate Effect)** | Return/loss from interest rate movements |
| **Credit Spread Effect** | Return/loss from spread widening/narrowing |
| **Selection Effect** | Return from picking specific bonds that outperformed/underperformed peers |
| **Roll-Down Return** | Gain from a bond's price naturally rising as it approaches maturity along the yield curve (assuming a normal upward-sloping curve) |

## 4.2 Relative Value Analysis

Rather than asking "is this a good bond," expert managers ask "is this bond **cheap or expensive relative to comparable alternatives**?" This involves:
- Comparing spreads across similarly-rated issuers in the same sector
- Analyzing the issuer's own bond curve (its 3-year vs. 5-year vs. 10-year bonds) for pricing anomalies
- Cross-market analysis (e.g., comparing a bond's spread to its Credit Default Swap pricing)

## 4.3 Derivatives & Hedging in Fixed Income

| Instrument | Institutional Use |
|---|---|
| **Interest Rate Swaps** | Convert fixed-rate exposure to floating (or vice versa) without selling the underlying bond |
| **Credit Default Swaps (CDS)** | Insurance-like contracts that pay out if an issuer defaults — used to hedge credit risk or take a view without owning the bond |
| **Bond Futures** | Used to quickly adjust portfolio duration without transacting in the cash bond market |
| **Options on Bonds/Futures** | Used to hedge tail risk or generate additional income (covered strategies) |

## 4.4 Regulatory & Structural Considerations

| Topic | Institutional Relevance |
|---|---|
| **Basel III / Capital Requirements** | Affects how banks price and hold corporate bonds |
| **SEBI Mutual Fund Regulations (India)** | Define permissible exposure limits, valuation norms (e.g., mark-to-market rules), and risk disclosures for debt funds |
| **Side-Pocketing** | A mechanism allowing funds to segregate distressed/defaulted assets from the main portfolio, protecting other investors from being penalized by one bad holding |
| **Stress Testing & Liquidity Risk Management** | Regulatory requirement for funds to model redemption stress scenarios against portfolio liquidity |

## 4.5 Macro-Driven Positioning

Expert-level fixed income management integrates macroeconomic views directly into portfolio decisions:

- **Central Bank Policy Reading** — anticipating rate decisions based on inflation data, employment data, and central bank communication ("forward guidance")
- **Cross-Asset Signals** — using equity market volatility (VIX), currency moves, and commodity trends to inform credit spread expectations
- **Global Capital Flows** — understanding how foreign institutional flows into/out of a country's corporate bond market affect local yields and spreads

## 4.6 ESG Integration in Credit Analysis

Modern institutional fixed income analysis increasingly incorporates:
- **Environmental risk** — exposure to transition risk (e.g., carbon-intensive industries facing regulatory change)
- **Governance risk** — management integrity, related-party transactions, accounting quality — often a leading indicator of credit deterioration
- **Green/Sustainability-Linked Bonds** — bonds with coupon structures tied to ESG performance targets

## 4.7 How Fund Managers Actually Use This

At the institutional level, individual bond-picking is just one input. I'm managing **portfolio-level risk budgets** — how much duration risk, how much credit risk, how much liquidity risk am I willing to run, given my fund's mandate and investor base? I use derivatives to fine-tune exposures without disrupting the core portfolio, and I'm constantly running relative value screens across hundreds of issuers to find mispriced opportunities before the broader market does. Governance red flags in credit analysis are often what alert me to a problem months before rating agencies act.
